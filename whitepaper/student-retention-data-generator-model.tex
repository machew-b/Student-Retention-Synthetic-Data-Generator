\vspace{6pt}

\noindent\textbf{Abstract.} Philippine higher-education records show what students do (grades, attendance, account status) but rarely why they leave. Published four-year attrition figures also disagree. A Commission on Higher Education (CHED) regional table reports two consecutive graduating cohorts whose regional rates differ by about 19 percentage points on average. The national figures of roughly 29\% and 39\% do not appear to trace to the same cohort or source, and no public dataset records the individual-level mechanisms behind either one. This paper specifies a synthetic data model that reproduces this gap on purpose. A latent state (structural vulnerability and exposure to acute shocks) drives both the observed record and the eventual dropout, and no downstream analyst can observe it. A confounded intervention-assignment mechanism, which mirrors how early-warning systems base support on the signals that predict the outcome, prevents a naive comparison from revealing a support program's effect. Every parameter carries a tag of locked, calibrated, or assumed. The model calibrates to Class of 2022 regional values, grouped into a high- and a typical-attrition tier, because the 39\% figure has no stated cohort or independent source. It uses the 29.4\% figure, which this paper reads as the Class of 2023 value, only for a held-out check kept separate from calibration. In a pilot, the intended confounding appears. Only estimators that borrow strength across signal cells recover the true effect, and the held-out check lands marginally outside its pre-specified band.
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

National figures on Philippine higher-education attrition describe a large problem but disagree on its size. Reported four-year cumulative attrition ranges from roughly three to four in ten students who begin a bachelor's degree, depending on the figure and the graduating cohort. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} traces each figure to its cohort and states which one the model calibrates to and why. National averages also hide wide regional spread. In the regional table of the Commission on Higher Education (CHED), cumulative attrition runs from 36.3\% to 93.4\% across regions for the Class of 2022. For the Class of 2023 it runs from 8.0\% to 38.4\%, and the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) is unreported \citep{cpbrd2026}. These figures aggregate by region, institution, or cohort. By design, they cannot describe the individual student whose circumstances led to a specific outcome.

Institutional records therefore miss much of what determines a student's trajectory. A registrar's database holds grades, attendance flags, and tuition-account status. It does not hold, and generally cannot lawfully or practically obtain, a reliable measure of a student's financial precarity or household stability. It also cannot show whether an acute event, such as a medical emergency, family crisis, or unexpected expense, has just made continued enrollment impossible. Qualitative research on Philippine dropout repeatedly identifies financial strain, family and employment pressures, and weak institutional support as dominant themes in students' own accounts \citep{cahilog2025,socioeconomic2024}. Yet administrative datasets almost never include these factors as columns.

The individual-level data needed to study this gap does not exist publicly, and for privacy reasons should not. Any retention early-warning system therefore faces a basic evidentiary problem, because no one, including the institution that generates the data, ever observes the ground truth for checking predictions. This paper takes a different route. It specifies a synthetic data model that treats the gap between recorded and true state as the central object of study, not as noise to engineer around.

The model separates each student's situation into two layers. The observed layer holds grades, attendance, financial-hold status, and whether the student received institutional support. Analysts can export and analyze it, and it resembles a real student-information system. The latent layer holds a fixed baseline vulnerability and a per-term probability of an acute shock. It drives the observed layer and the eventual dropout, but the generator never exports it, so no analysis of the dataset can see it. This deliberate gap reproduces the position of a Student Success Analyst who works from institutional records alone. Philippine and international higher-education administration increasingly uses this title for the staff role that owns retention analytics and intervention targeting, although it is not yet a standardized job title. Here it stands in for whichever office owns that responsibility.

This paper contributes the data model itself. The model comprises the entity structure, the latent-to-observed generative logic, the calibration targets from published Philippine statistics, and the validation checks that any implementation must satisfy. The generator's source code and the resulting dataset are separate deliverables built on this specification. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the motivating literature. Section~\ref{sec:principles} states the design principles, and Section~\ref{sec:architecture} describes the entity inventory and pipeline. Sections~\ref{sec:phase1}--\ref{sec:phase5} detail the five phases. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} specifies the calibration parameters and provenance tags. Section~\ref{sec:validation} gives the validation measures and benchmarks, and Section~\ref{sec:edgecases} describes two engineered edge cases. Sections~\ref{sec:ethics}, \ref{sec:limitations}, and~\ref{sec:conclusion} cover ethics, limitations, and conclusions. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA} gives a reference instantiation and pilot check, and Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixB} gives the field-level schema and reproducibility policy.

\section{Related Work and Theoretical Foundation}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Classical Attrition Theory}
\label{sec:related-A}

Tinto's model frames dropout as the end point of insufficient academic and social integration into an institution. It remains the most widely cited theoretical account of college attrition \citep{tinto1975}. Bean and Metzner extend it to non-traditional students. They argue that external factors (finances, employment, family responsibility) dominate campus integration for students who are older, commute, or work while enrolled \citep{bean1985}. Both accounts support the claim that motivates this paper. The recordable events that precede a dropout, such as a missed class, a failed course, or a lapsed payment, are downstream symptoms of a state that no record captures. The two-layer structure of Section~\ref{sec:phase2} operationalizes this claim.

\subsection{Machine-Learning Dropout Prediction and the Actionability Gap}
\label{sec:related-B}

A large and growing literature applies machine-learning classifiers to institutional and learning-management-system data to predict dropout, and it frequently reports high accuracy. A 2026 systematic review of 75 quality-assessed studies, conducted under the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, finds limited evidence that these models produce empirically validated interventions in real institutions. The review calls this shortfall the actionability gap \citep{dropout2026review}. An accurate risk prediction does not show that a program reduces risk. This paper's data model therefore treats prediction and intervention effectiveness as two questions, each with its own independently checkable answer.

\subsection{Confounded Treatment Assignment in Educational Interventions}
\label{sec:related-C}

The actionability gap has a specific statistical cause when institutions target support at students already identified as at risk, which is standard and sensible practice. Smith and Chimedza show by simulation that a naive treated-versus-untreated comparison is biased when treatment assignment depends on the variables that predict the outcome. They add that the bias can be verified only when the true effect is known by construction \citep{smith2021}. This paper adopts that logic. Section~\ref{sec:phase4} specifies an assignment mechanism confounded by prior-term observed risk. Section~\ref{sec:phase5} fixes a true intervention effect as a design constant. Any downstream causal analysis then has a known correct answer to check against.

\subsection{The Philippine Empirical Landscape}
\label{sec:related-D}

Three government and quasi-government sources anchor the calibration targets, and this paper states which figures come from which report. The House of Representatives' Congressional Policy and Budget Research Department (CPBRD) reproduces CHED's regional attrition figures for two consecutive graduating cohorts in its FY2026 Agency Budget Notes for CHED, with enrollment and institution counts per region \citep{cpbrd2026}. The Second Congressional Commission on Education (EDCOM~II) published a Year One report in 2024. That report covers, among other findings, the targeting of tertiary subsidies, and it is not the source of the attrition figures used here. EDCOM~II's Year Two report (2025) is the source of the national and regional figures, namely about 39\% nationally plus figures for BARMM, Region VII, Region IX, and the Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR). CPBRD's Agency Budget Notes later reproduce these figures in its own narrative account of a legislator's floor speech, not as a CPBRD-computed statistic \citep{edcom2024,edcom2025y2}. This provenance chain is central to resolving the national-attrition discrepancy in Section~\ref{sec:calibration}. It is also why this paper does not treat the EDCOM~II figure and the CPBRD-quoted figure as independent corroboration. The Philippine Institute for Development Studies (PIDS) independently confirms the pandemic-era rise in national attrition and places it within broader concerns about CHED's regulatory and developmental capacity \citep{pids2024}. Institutional qualitative work also identifies drivers of dropout. A 2025 phenomenological study of dropped-out students at a tuition-free public higher education institution (HEI) names financial struggles, family obligations, low academic readiness, and weak institutional support \citep{cahilog2025}, and state-university research reports similar socioeconomic correlates \citep{socioeconomic2024}. Together these sources establish the aggregate scale of the phenomenon and its qualitative drivers. None provides the individual-level record that would let a researcher observe the hidden and recorded states of the same student at once, and none could.

\section{Design Principles}
\label{sec:principles}

Five principles govern the modeling decisions in the following sections, so each choice can be checked against a stated intention.

\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{Empirical Fidelity.} Every aggregate quantity that the generator produces and that has a real published counterpart (a national attrition rate, a regional split, a driver's relative prevalence) must reproduce that counterpart within ±3 percentage points (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), and the counterpart's source must be citable. Two quantities follow different rules (Sections~\ref{sec:calibration} and~\ref{sec:validation}). The pooled national rate derives from the tier targets and is not matched to a national headline figure, and the held-out tier ratio has its own pre-specified band.
\item \textbf{Deliberate Opacity.} The model represents the gap between what is recorded and what is true directly, instead of leaving it as an incompleteness. A downstream analyst who works only from the exported tables therefore faces the same informational limits as a real Student Success Analyst (Section~\ref{sec:intro}).
\item \textbf{Full Traceability.} Every parameter carries a tag stating that it is fixed by design, calibrated against a cited source, or explicitly assumed. No parameter may carry an unstated status.
\item \textbf{Built-In Falsifiability.} The model fixes at least one quantity, the true effect of the support intervention, that no real dataset can reveal but that the model knows by construction. A downstream causal analysis can then be scored against a correct answer, not only a plausible-looking one.
\item \textbf{Bounded Scope.} The model limits granularity to what the cited statistics support. Where a real number exists only at the national or two-tier regional level, the model does not manufacture false precision by fitting 17 individual regions to it.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Architecture and End-to-End Pipeline}
\label{sec:architecture}

Six of the model's eight tables are panels with one row per student per academic term. Regions and Students are fixed at generation time. The choice is substantive. The question ``will this student leave next term'' concerns a sequence of states, so the data must contain the sequence as a sequence. The person-period (discrete-time panel) representation is standard for discrete-time survival analysis. A term is the unit of time, and a dropout is either an observed event or, for students still enrolled at the end of the observation window, a right-censored non-event.

Generation proceeds through five phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Phases 1--4 and the per-term draw in Phase 5 (Phase 5a) run in fixed order over each student's full enrollment history. Phase 5b checks aggregate output against real benchmarks. It operates on the batch and can run only after all $N$ students exist. Its results recalibrate the numeric parameters from Phases 1 and 2 before the generator is final (the dashed feedback path in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). This closed loop of parameter setting, output generation, and comparison with real benchmarks follows standard input-modeling and verification-and-validation practice for discrete-event and Monte Carlo simulation. That practice accepts a model only after its output has matched real-world reference data \citep{law2015}. Because the loop tunes parameters against the same benchmarks that later check them, it is calibration-until-convergence, not independent held-out validation (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

The specification calls for 50,000 students [LOCKED; a scale decision, not a calibrated one], each followed for up to eight terms. This size keeps the tier-level validation check of Section~\ref{sec:validation} statistically meaningful, although the high-attrition tier holds only about 15\% of the population (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). That tier still contains roughly 7,500 students. A binomial back-of-envelope calculation puts the sampling error of a simulated attrition rate near 67\% at about half a percentage point ($\sqrt{0.67 \times 0.33 / 7{,}500} \approx 0.0054$). This is an order-of-magnitude check, not a precise bound. CPBRD enrollment data place the 15\% tier share within a citable range (Table~\ref{tab:params}) but pin it down only to 14.1\% to 18\%, so a two-decimal error figure would overstate precision. The robust conclusion is that sampling noise at $N=50{,}000$ is an order of magnitude smaller than the $\pm$3-percentage-point tolerance of Section~\ref{sec:validation} across that whole range. A validation failure at that threshold would therefore reflect genuine miscalibration, not sampling noise.

\section{Cohort and Region Initialization (Phase 1)}
\label{sec:phase1}

Every synthetic student begins with fixed attributes drawn independently of any later state. These attributes are region tier, entry year, program discipline, household income bracket, working-student status, distance from campus, and subsidy status at entry. Region tier matters most, because it connects the model to the largest and most reliable real benchmark available, published regional attrition figures.

Reported four-year cumulative attrition varies widely across regions within each cohort. CPBRD's regional table reports 36.3\% to 93.4\% for the Class of 2022 and 8.0\% to 38.4\% for the Class of 2023 (BARMM unreported). Attrition falls by about 19 percentage points on average per region from the first cohort to the second \citep{cpbrd2026}. Modeling every region would require region-specific calibration data that this project lacks, and it would imply a precision that the sources cannot support. The model therefore groups regions into two tiers. The high-attrition tier comprises the four regions with the highest Class of 2022 cumulative attrition in CPBRD's table \citep{cpbrd2026}. These regions are BARMM at 93.4\%, Region VII at 60.7\%, Region IX at 59.5\%, and CAR at 54.9\%, all well above the 36.3\% of Region II, which sits at the low end of that column. The typical tier comprises all remaining regions (13, all with reported values). The grouping is a deliberately coarse stand-in for region-level random effects nested within a national distribution \citep{gelman2013bda}, a scope decision that Section~\ref{sec:limitations} revisits. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} gives each tier's numeric target.

Region IX needs a note. CPBRD's infographic table gives 59.5\%, while the same document's narrative text gives 59.9\% when it reports a legislator's floor speech. This paper did not create that source-internal inconsistency and resolves it by using the tabulated figure throughout (see Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). EDCOM~II's Year Two report reproduces the four tabulated figures exactly and does not contain the 59.9\% figure at all \citep{edcom2025y2}. This strengthens confidence in the tabulated regional figures, though, as Section~\ref{sec:calibration} discusses, not in the national total.

\section{Latent State Assignment (Phase 2)}
\label{sec:phase2}

This phase generates two quantities. The generator never exports either one to any table, and no analysis of the dataset can access them. They are the true, unobserved cause of everything that follows.

Baseline vulnerability is a continuous value fixed once per student at entry as a function of region tier, income bracket, and working-student status. It represents structural exposure to the pressures that Section~\ref{sec:related} identifies as dominant drivers of Philippine attrition, namely financial precarity and competing employment demands. No real student-information system records those pressures directly, and no analyst can observe this value either.

Term shock is a per-term indicator, redrawn independently every term with probability $\pi_s$ (Table~\ref{tab:params}). It represents an acute disruption uncorrelated with the student's baseline standing, such as a medical event, a family emergency, or an unplanned expense. This independence is a load-bearing design choice. It lets a student with low baseline vulnerability and no adverse recorded history still leave, which is the mechanism behind the first edge case in Section~\ref{sec:edgecases}. Fig.~\ref{fig:dag} shows the causal structure that connects these two latent quantities to every observed and derived quantity in Sections~\ref{sec:phase3}--\ref{sec:phase5}. It follows the probabilistic graphical model (PGM) convention of the Bayesian modeling literature, which distinguishes random variables from deterministic functions and observed from latent quantities \citep{mcelreath2020,gelman2013bda}.

\section{Term-by-Term Observable Generation (Phase 3)}
\label{sec:phase3}

Term grade, attendance rate, and financial-hold status are each a noisy function of the current baseline vulnerability and the current term's shock. The noise is functionally necessary. It keeps the observed variables from fully revealing the latent state, which makes the downstream analyst's problem nontrivial instead of a disguised lookup. Two students with identical baseline vulnerability and shock status will generally present differently on these variables, and two students who present identically will generally differ in true vulnerability.

The three variables are independent of each other conditional on the latent state, but they correlate unconditionally because they share the same latent cause. This mirrors real administrative data, where attendance and financial-hold status often correlate mildly. One does not cause the other. A common, unrecorded circumstance drives both.

\section{Confounded Intervention Assignment (Phase 4)}
\label{sec:phase4}

Throughout this section and Section~\ref{sec:phase5}, $i = 1,\ldots,N$ indexes students ($N=50{,}000$, Table~\ref{tab:entities}) and $t = 1,\ldots,T_i$ indexes terms. The bound $T_i \le 8$ is the term in which student $i$ drops out or, for students still enrolled, the last term of the observation window (the right-censoring point of Section~\ref{sec:architecture}). The function $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ is the standard logistic function. It maps any real input to $(0,1)$ so the output can be read as a probability.

The model computes an observed risk score from the prior term's grade, attendance, and financial-hold values. It never uses the current term's values or the latent state. An institution that decides whether to intervene this term cannot see this term's outcomes or the latent state, and the mechanism faces the same constraint.

Term 1 has no prior term, so no risk score exists. Eqs.~\ref{eq:risk}--\ref{eq:intervention} therefore apply only for $t \ge 2$, and $\text{intervention}_{i,1}=0$ for every student. No support decision rests on a record that does not yet exist. The model deliberately does not substitute entry attributes (Section~\ref{sec:phase1}) as a term-1 input. Doing so would add a second path into assignment outside the three prior-term signals and would change what an adjusted analysis must condition on (Table~\ref{tab:validation}). This is a simplification, because a real institution may act on entry information and this version does not represent that. Three consequences follow. A student's first term is always untreated, so the term-1 hazard in Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard} carries no intervention factor. At most seven of eight terms are eligible for support. And every statement below about targeting rates, the eight-valued score, and the Confounding Presence and Recoverability benchmarks refers to student-terms with $t \ge 2$.

One defensible form for the score is a logistic combination of prior-term indicators.

\begin{equation}
\text{risk}_{i,t} \;=\; \sigma\!\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \mathbf{1}[\text{grade}_{i,t-1} < \tau_g] + \beta_2 \mathbf{1}[\text{attendance}_{i,t-1} < \tau_a] + \beta_3\, \text{hold}_{i,t-1}\right), \quad \beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3 > 0
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}

Here $\text{hold}_{i,t-1}$ is the prior term's binary financial-hold flag. The generator enforces the sign constraint $\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3 > 0$. The direction of the risk relationship is therefore a mathematical property of Eq.~\ref{eq:risk}, not a prose claim. A low grade, low attendance, or an active hold each independently \emph{raises} risk. The thresholds $\tau_g$ and $\tau_a$ mark what counts as low. They take illustrative values in Table~\ref{tab:params} and, like the three weights and $\beta_0$, are ASSUMED. Eq.~\ref{eq:risk} is the only functional form that the main text gives for a quantity derived from Phase 3. The architecture leaves open the forms for grade, attendance, and hold (Section~\ref{sec:phase3}), and Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA} gives one admissible instantiation. This lets the main text argue the architecture without serving as an implementation manual.

Support this term is a Bernoulli trial with $\text{risk}_{i,t}$ as the success probability.

\begin{equation}
\text{intervention}_{i,t} \mid \text{risk}_{i,t} \;\sim\; \text{Bernoulli}\!\left(\text{risk}_{i,t}\right)
\label{eq:intervention}
\end{equation}

Assignment is stochastic, not a hard threshold rule that flags a student when $\text{risk}_{i,t}$ exceeds a cutoff $\theta$, because the score is discrete. All three inputs to Eq.~\ref{eq:risk} are binary, so $\text{risk}_{i,t}$ takes only $2^3=8$ values. The score exists for up to 350,000 student-terms (50,000 students $\times$ up to seven terms from $t=2$). The true count is somewhat smaller after right-censoring, because not every student reaches term 8 (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}). A hard cutoff can only partition those eight buckets, so it cannot hit an exact targeting rate except by coincidence of the population proportions. A stochastic draw can hit a target rate \emph{in expectation} by giving each bucket its own intervention probability, which Eq.~\ref{eq:intervention} does implicitly through $\sigma(\cdot)$. The model needs no threshold $\theta$.

A question of identification remains. The score has six free quantities, $\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3,\tau_g,\tau_a$, but only two aggregate targets exist. They are a targeting sensitivity of approximately 70\% and a false-flag rate of approximately 25\% (defined below). The system is underdetermined, and no solution is privileged without an additional assumption. This paper adds one explicit, ASSUMED simplification, namely that the three weights are equal, $\beta_1=\beta_2=\beta_3=\beta$, because no prior-term signal has a principled claim to more trust than the others at this scope. The paper also fixes $\tau_g$ and $\tau_a$ at their illustrative Table~\ref{tab:params} values, independently of the targeting calculation, because they define what ``low'' means and not how strongly a low reading counts. Two free parameters, $\beta_0$ and $\beta$, then face two targets. Table~\ref{tab:worked} and the discussion after it show what this does and does not pin down.

To confirm that this is more than a labeling exercise, Table~\ref{tab:worked} works an illustrative case. The case checks only that Eqs.~\ref{eq:risk}--\ref{eq:intervention} \emph{can} reproduce the targeting figures. Suppose that 30\% of student-terms are genuinely high-current-vulnerability. This is an illustrative split, not a Table~\ref{tab:params} parameter, because the real joint distribution of grade, attendance, and hold depends on the Phase 3 instantiation. Suppose also that each of the three binary signals fires independently with probability 0.65 in that group and 0.20 otherwise. Let $k \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ be the number of signals that fire for a student-term. Under equal weights, $\text{risk}$ depends only on $k$. Choosing $(\beta_0,\beta)$ so that the flagged population comes close to both targets gives, for example, $\beta_0 \approx -9$ and $\beta \approx 6$, as Table~\ref{tab:worked} shows.

Averaging $\text{risk}(k)$ against these two distributions gives a targeting sensitivity of approximately 71\% and a false-flag rate of approximately 28\%. Both figures sit close to the 70\% and 25\% design targets, and the Phase 5b calibration loop (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) would fine-tune them. Hitting both targets exactly is possible, but only with a near-step assignment, $\text{risk}(0) \approx \text{risk}(1) \approx 0$, $\text{risk}(2) \approx 0.96$, and $\text{risk}(3) \approx 1$. Any sufficiently large $\beta$ reproduces this step with $\beta_0 \approx 3.15 - 2\beta$ (for example $\beta \approx 10$ or $\beta \approx 37$). In this submodel the two targets therefore fix the assignment probabilities at $k=2$ and $k=3$, not a unique $(\beta_0,\beta)$. The steepness remains a free degree of freedom, which the smoother pair in Table~\ref{tab:worked} resolves by choice.

The exercise also yields a finding, not only a confirmation. The calibrated $\text{risk}(k)$ values come close to a step function, near zero for $k \le 1$ and near one for $k \ge 2$. This follows directly from hitting precise aggregate targets with a score that has only eight possible values, the discreteness problem that Section~\ref{sec:limitations} notes.

A second consequence concerns identification. Because $\text{risk}(0) < 0.01$ and $\text{risk}(3) > 0.99$, assignment is nearly deterministic when no signal fires or all three fire. Treated terms in the $k=0$ cell, and untreated terms in the $k=3$ cell, occur at roughly 1 in 8,000, since $\sigma(-9) \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-4}$. Only the one- and two-signal cells therefore contain both treated and untreated student-terms. This is a near-violation of the positivity (overlap) condition that any adjusted analysis must respect (Section~\ref{sec:validation}; Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA} reports pilot cell counts). A formally stochastic mechanism does not remove the problem. It only makes the targets achievable in expectation instead of by coincidence, while most variation across the eight buckets stays at the extremes.

The targeting is imperfect by design. Approximately 70\% of students with genuinely high current vulnerability receive correct identification and an offer of support. Approximately 25\% of the students offered support have genuinely low underlying vulnerability and received a flag because of a single weak term. This imperfection creates the confounding of Section~\ref{sec:related-C} \citep{smith2021}. Assignment correlates with true risk but does not match it, so a naive comparison of supported and unsupported students misestimates the program's effect. In observational-study terms, the design violates the ignorable-treatment-assignment assumption that a simple group comparison needs to identify a causal effect \citep{gelman2020ros}. A corrected analysis must condition away exactly this confounding path in the causal-inference sense \citep{peters2017}. This is the failure mode that the dataset's intervention-effectiveness component lets an analyst discover and correct.

\section{Outcome Generation and Validation (Phase 5)}
\label{sec:phase5}

Section~\ref{sec:architecture} frames dropout as a discrete-time hazard problem. This section states the hazard equation, the paper's most important functional form, because it generates the target variable. Dropout in a given term is a Bernoulli trial whose success probability is a discrete-time hazard. The hazard is a deterministic logistic function of baseline vulnerability, that term's shock, and whether the student received support this term.

\begin{align}
h_{i,t} &= \sigma\!\left(\gamma_0 + \gamma_1\, v_i + \gamma_2\, \text{shock}_{i,t}\right)\times\left(1 - 0.30 \cdot \text{intervention}_{i,t}\right), \qquad \gamma_1,\gamma_2 > 0 \label{eq:hazard}\\
\text{dropout}_{i,t} \mid h_{i,t} &\sim \text{Bernoulli}(h_{i,t}) \label{eq:dropout}
\end{align}

These equations apply to students still enrolled at the start of term $t$ (that is, $\text{dropout}_{i,t'}=0$ for all $t'<t$). Here $v_i$ is baseline vulnerability standardized to a continuous scale, and $\text{shock}_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ is that term's shock indicator (Section~\ref{sec:phase2}).

Unlike $\beta_0$ and $\beta$ in Section~\ref{sec:phase4}, $\gamma_0,\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ receive no point values here. Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard}'s three parameters face the same underdetermination that Section~\ref{sec:phase4} resolved for Eq.~\ref{eq:risk}'s six, and the architecture offers no analogous assumption. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA} resolves it by explicit choice.

The two independent calibration targets are the tier-level cumulative attrition rates in Table~\ref{tab:params}, both from the Class of 2022. They are about 67\% for the high tier and about 46\% for the typical tier. The pooled national figure, about 49\% at the 15\% tier share, is their share-weighted blend and not a third constraint. Two numbers cannot identify three parameters without a further assumption. The equal-weighting assumption of Section~\ref{sec:phase4} has no analogue here. The coefficient $\gamma_1$ applies to continuous standardized vulnerability and $\gamma_2$ applies to a binary shock, so the two are not structurally interchangeable, and fixing their ratio would lack motivation.

A third kind of target would resolve the remaining degree of freedom, namely a term-by-term hazard shape (whether risk within a tier concentrates early, late, or spreads roughly uniformly). The cumulative figures of Section~\ref{sec:calibration} describe only the four-year total, and no source cited in Section~\ref{sec:related} reports Philippine attrition at term level. That target is therefore unavailable. The Phase 1/2 feedback loop (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) fits $(\gamma_0,\gamma_1,\gamma_2)$ through a multi-term simulation calibration against the two tier targets. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA} resolves the remaining degree of freedom by fixing $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ as ASSUMED values and calibrating $\gamma_0$, together with the tier shift in baseline vulnerability, to the two targets. This is one admissible resolution, not one that the architecture requires.

The $0.30$ factor in Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard} applies to every student-term with $\text{intervention}_{i,t}=1$. It is an internal design constant (LOCKED, Table~\ref{tab:params}), not a claim about any real intervention's effectiveness. A downstream causal analysis therefore has a specific, known value to recover instead of a plausible range to argue for. The equation has no compliance or fidelity gate. The model does not distinguish an administered intervention that ``genuinely'' helped from one that did not, and every supported student-term receives the same $0.30$ reduction by construction. The 30\% reduction is thus a fixed, population-average effect, not a per-student heterogeneous effect drawn from a further latent distribution. This keeps the Recoverability benchmark in Table~\ref{tab:validation} well posed, because ``recovers an estimate close to the true effect'' has one unambiguous target. A heterogeneous-effects version is a recognized and active extension of this simulation-based validation approach \citep{smith2020global}. In that version, a correctly specified analysis must recover a conditional average treatment effect instead of a single number. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} leaves it as future work.

This project located no real Philippine study of institutional retention-support interventions that would allow calibrating the 30\% magnitude the way Table~\ref{tab:params} calibrates the attrition figures. The value therefore remains ASSUMED in spirit despite the LOCKED tag. LOCKED means fixed by design for internal consistency, not empirically settled. As a rough plausibility check, not a calibration source, a 2024 meta-analysis of dropout-intervention programs finds effect sizes of about 0.31 for combined interventions and 0.33 for behavioral interventions \citep{wang2024meta}. That study covers K--12 and reports an effect size, not a hazard-ratio reduction, so the comparison holds only at the order-of-magnitude level. The effects are modest rather than transformative, which matches the register of a 30\% relative hazard reduction. A 30\% reduction is large enough to give the Recoverability benchmark a checkable signal, but not so large that an analyst could recover it by accident from noise. A closer match on the metric, though not the population, is a randomized trial of a middle-school relevance intervention. Its event-history analysis found a 23\% reduction in the hazard of later high-school dropout \citep{rose2021careerstart}. That trial also covers K--12 and is a single study. This paper does not claim that these figures compare with the effect of a higher-education support program. It claims only that 30\% is a plausible order of magnitude for this literature. The value remains a design choice made for the validation goal of Section~\ref{sec:validation}, not an empirical estimate.

At the end of each student's observed history, the generator rolls the term-level dropout flags into one cohort-level attrition flag, which matches the published four-year cumulative figures in definition (Section~\ref{sec:phase1}). The validation step checks this rolled-up quantity, not the per-term hazard, against the real benchmarks in Table~\ref{tab:validation}. Per-term mechanics stay internal, but aggregate behavior must match cited reality within ±3 percentage points (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) before the parameter set is final. Otherwise the generator revises the Phase 1 and Phase 2 parameters and regenerates the cohort (the feedback path in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}).

CPBRD's FY2026 Agency Budget Notes for CHED contain two national attrition figures. They can look like competing estimates of one quantity. Tracing each figure to its origin and graduating cohort shows that they are not. It also shows that pairing one cohort's national figure with another cohort's regional figures would mix cohorts. This section resolves the discrepancy and shows its reasoning.

\emph{The $\approx$39\% figure.} Press coverage repeats this figure most often, and it appears in both CPBRD's document and an EDCOM~II report, which might make it look well corroborated. Tracing it removes that appearance. Within the CPBRD document, a whole cluster of figures built around 39\% is not a CPBRD-computed statistic. The cluster is a national figure of about 39\%, BARMM at 93.4\%, Region VII at 60.7\%, Region IX at 59.9\%, and CAR at 54.9\%. It appears in a narrative paragraph that CPBRD attributes to Representative Jude Acidre's privilege speech of September 1, 2025 \citep{cpbrd2026}. The Highlights section repeats the same sentences without attribution. The passage has no quotation marks and reads as CPBRD's account of what the speech said. This paper therefore describes it as CPBRD reporting the speech's content, not quoting it. This project located no transcript of the speech to confirm word-for-word agreement. The same cluster, with Region IX at 59.5\%, already appears in EDCOM~II's Year Two report of January 2025, eight months before the speech \citep{edcom2025y2}, and Representative Acidre is an EDCOM~II commissioner. The most parsimonious reading is that the speech restates EDCOM~II's own figure. A legislator who cites a report he co-authored is not a second corroborating source. The 59.9\% Region IX figure in the narrative cluster also differs from the 59.5\% in CPBRD's own regional table for the same region and cohort (Section~\ref{sec:phase1}). This gives a second reason to treat the narrative cluster with more caution than the tabulated data.

\emph{The 29.4\% figure.} This figure appears in CPBRD's infographic table as the national total. CPBRD attributes it explicitly and only to the ``CHED Office of Planning, Research, and Knowledge Management'' \citep{cpbrd2026}, CHED's primary internal data source, so it has a short, direct, named chain of custody. The table's attrition column carries the heading ``Class of '23 ('22)''. Each region lists the Class of 2023 value first and the Class of 2022 value in parentheses, and the national row lists a single unparenthesized value. This paper reads that value as the Class of 2023 national figure. The table's layout implies this reading, but no label prints it. An independent check supports it. Weight each region's value by SY~2023--24 enrollment from the same table, as a proxy for cohort size, and exclude BARMM because neither its enrollment nor its Class of 2023 value is reported. The Class of 2023 column then averages 27.4\%, within about two points of 29.4\%, whereas the Class of 2022 column averages 47.8\%.

The same table shows that the Class of 2022 regional values quoted alongside 39\% do not reproduce it. The 16 reported values average 47.8\% enrollment-weighted and 47.3\% unweighted, about nine points above 39\%. Neither CPBRD nor EDCOM~II states the batch or base population of the 39\%.

Two further sources bear on cohort. CHED's FY2026 budget presentation to the House reports 29.39\% bachelor's-degree attrition for academic year (AY) 2023--24 \citep{ched2025presentation}. This matches the CPBRD national total and supports reading it as the more recent cohort. A press report of CHED data given to the House in 2023 gives national attrition of 38.95\% for the SY 2021--22 graduating batch and 41.16\% for the 2023 batch. It notes that CHED defines attrition as students who temporarily leave or drop out permanently \citep{chi2023pandemic}. The first of these is a candidate underlying source for the 39\% figure. Neither CPBRD nor EDCOM~II cites it, however, and CPBRD's ``Class of '22'' label does not tie to a school year. These documents therefore cannot establish whether the 39\% and the Class of 2022 regional values describe the same batch. The chain of custody for 39\% in CPBRD and EDCOM~II remains indirect, running from a floor speech to a commission report that does not cite its source for the number.

\emph{Resolution.} The model uses neither national figure as a Class of 2022 target. It calibrates by tier instead, and both tier targets come from the same cohort and column of the same table (Table~\ref{tab:params}). The high-attrition tier targets about 67\% and the typical tier about 46\%. The pooled national rate is a derived quantity, about 49\% at the assumed 15\% tier share. The paper reports it but does not match it to an external figure. The only national figure found quoted alongside the Class of 2022 regional values is the 39\% figure, so the derived rate sits about ten percentage points above it. This gap is a known, unresolved consequence of calibrating to the regional table instead of a national headline. Reproducing 39\% with a 15\% high tier at about 67\% would require a typical-tier rate of about 34\% (33\% to 34\% across the 14.1\% to 18\% share range). That rate falls below every reported Class of 2022 regional value, the lowest of which is 36.3\%.

The model retains the 29.4\% figure, as a Class of 2023 figure, only to fix the overall level in the held-out check of Section~\ref{sec:validation}. The paper logs the 39\% figure for transparency. It is not a calibration target, and repetition across CPBRD and EDCOM~II does not corroborate it, because those sources are not independent. The choice matters. Blending a Class of 2022 high tier at about 67\% with the Class of 2023 national figure would imply a typical-tier target of about 23\%. No Class of 2022 regional value supports that target, since the lowest is 36.3\%. The blend would also imply a high-to-typical ratio of about 2.9, where the Class of 2022 column supports about 1.5 (67.1\% to 45.9\%). A future revision should revisit this resolution if CHED source data with a documented cohort definition and base population for each national figure become available.

\emph{BARMM sensitivity.} The high-tier target of 67.1\% is a simple mean in which BARMM carries a quarter of the weight, because the table does not report BARMM's enrollment. The three high-tier regions with reported enrollment average 59.2\% over 725,163 students. Adding BARMM at 93.4\% to an enrollment-weighted mean gives 60.4\% if BARMM enrolls 25,000 students, 63.4\% at 100,000, 65.1\% at 150,000, and 68.0\% at 250,000. For any BARMM enrollment below about 121,000, the weighted target falls below the lower edge of the $\pm$3-point band around 67.1\% (64.1\%). The high-to-typical ratio would then sit nearer 1.3 to 1.4 than 1.5. The simple mean stays because it uses only the four cited regional values and needs no BARMM enrollment figure. It coincides with the enrollment-weighted mean only if BARMM enrolls about 218,000 students, which this paper has no data to confirm or rule out. The enrollment-weighted alternative is therefore a candidate for the sensitivity analysis that the tagging above calls for.

\emph{Estimand.} In Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard} the intervention multiplies the per-term dropout probability by $0.70$. The true effect is therefore a risk ratio (equivalently, a ratio of discrete-time hazards) of exactly $0.70$, and ``30\%'' in Table~\ref{tab:validation} means $1-RR$. Within any stratum of the prior-term signals, and under any common standardization across strata, the ratio is the same. Assignment is independent of the latent state given those signals, so both arms share the same baseline hazard. The true effect is not an odds ratio, and the treatment coefficient of a logistic regression does not estimate it. The odds ratio implied by a fixed risk ratio of $0.70$ drifts away from $0.70$ as the baseline hazard rises. It equals about $0.67$ at a per-term hazard of $0.13$, roughly the average implied by 67\% cumulative attrition over eight terms under a constant-hazard approximation. It equals about $0.64$ at a hazard of $0.25$, an apparent 36\% reduction that already falls outside the 5-point tolerance. The Recoverability benchmark therefore scores the risk ratio.

An adjusted analysis should use an estimator that targets the risk ratio directly and borrows strength across the prior-signal cells. Examples are log-binomial or Poisson regression with robust standard errors, and a Mantel--Haenszel risk ratio across those cells. Inverse-probability weighting and full-population standardization are unsuitable. Assignment is nearly deterministic in two of the four signal-count cells (Section~\ref{sec:phase4}), so their weights become extreme (up to about 8,000), and the cells they must extrapolate into contain almost no observations of one arm. A logistic model works only if model-based standardization converts its fitted probabilities back to a risk ratio. The effect is identified only from cells where both arms are populated, so a correct estimator's sampling variability at $N = 50{,}000$ can approach the 5-point window in Table~\ref{tab:validation}. Replicated runs, not assumption, therefore set that window. In the pilot of Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA}, the estimator's standard deviation is about 4.4 points.

The national- and tier-level attrition checks are calibration-until-convergence, not independent validation. The feedback path of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) tunes Phase 1 and Phase 2 parameters against exactly these benchmarks. Passing them therefore shows that the tuning converged. It does not show that the model would reproduce a real statistic it was not tuned against, which is what held-out validation must show. A genuinely held-out check compares calibrated output against a reference figure that tuning never used. This specification pre-specifies such a check, and Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA} reports it for a pilot instantiation, not a production generator. The reference is the Class of 2023 column of the same CPBRD table \citep{cpbrd2026}, which supplies no tier target. The Class of 2022 column is the calibration cohort. The check has three steps. First, calibrate to the Class of 2022 tier targets as above. Second, freeze every calibrated parameter except the overall hazard level, and refit that level until pooled simulated attrition falls within 1 percentage point of 29.4\%, the Class of 2023 national figure. The overall hazard level is the intercept $\gamma_0$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard}, which belongs to the Phase 5 hazard but which the same feedback loop fits. Third, without further tuning, compute the simulated ratio of high-tier to typical-tier cumulative attrition and require it to lie between 1.2 and 1.7.

The reference values are as follows. In the Class of 2023 column, the three high-tier regions with a reported value (CAR, Region VII, and Region IX, since BARMM is unreported) average 35.7\%, and the 13 typical regions average 26.0\%, both enrollment-weighted. The ratio is 1.37. On the same three regions the Class of 2022 ratio is 1.29, so the ratio moved by 0.08 between cohorts. Including BARMM (with the simple four-region mean as the tier target) raises the Class of 2022 ratio from 1.29 to 1.46, which indicates how much the missing BARMM value could matter. The band runs from 1.37 less 0.15 (about twice the observed drift) to 1.37 plus 0.17 (the BARMM effect) plus 0.15, rounded to 1.2 and 1.7. In the sense of Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, the band edges and the 1-point refit tolerance are ASSUMED. The check targets this ratio, not the level, because levels are unstable across cohorts. Region-level attrition fell by about 19 percentage points on average between the two cohorts, with a standard deviation of 8.6 points across the 16 regions that report both values and a rank correlation of only 0.39. A level comparison at the $\pm$3-point tolerance used elsewhere would therefore measure cohort-to-cohort volatility, not model fidelity.

Two limits deserve explicit statement. First, the check has little power. It predicts one structural quantity after fitting one level parameter, and the band is wide, so passing is weak evidence of generalization while failing is informative. Second, the check is not blind. While designing the check, the author inspected the Class of 2023 values to compute the reference ratio and the drift above. The check is therefore held out from parameter fitting but not from design choices. The CPBRD table reports only two cohorts, so this source offers no further held-out cohort. In the pilot instantiation, the check lands marginally outside the band (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA}). The Confounding Presence and Recoverability checks do not share this calibration-loop limitation. Design fixes the true 30\% effect (Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard}), and the data being checked do not determine it. Comparing how a naive and an adjusted estimator recover it is therefore an independent check in the ordinary sense.

The exported target variable has one row per student per term, with a binary dropout flag and an end-of-window censoring indicator. It supports two complementary downstream analyses in a companion deliverable. One is supervised classification of the per-term dropout flag, of the kind catalogued in the statistical-learning literature \citep{james2013}. The other is a discrete-time hazard formulation built on the person-period structure of Section~\ref{sec:architecture} and Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard}. This specification performs neither analysis, and the data model keeps both available.

\section{Deliberate Edge Cases}
\label{sec:edgecases}

Two edge cases are required outputs of the generative mechanism, not optional extras.

\begin{enumerate}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{\textit{Unflagged high-risk student.}} This student has strong prior-term grades, good prior-term attendance, and no financial hold on record. The student therefore has a low observed risk score and receives no intervention this term. Yet the student drops out because a term shock occurred that no institution could have acted on in time. This term's record does not hide the shock. Per Section~\ref{sec:phase3}, the shock also shapes this term's grade, attendance, and hold values, so in hindsight the data partly reveals it. The intervention decision cannot see the shock, because Eq.~\ref{eq:risk} conditions only on the \emph{prior} term's values (Section~\ref{sec:phase4}) and the model computes it before this term's shock and its consequences exist. This case follows directly from the independence between term shock and the prior term's observed variables (Section~\ref{sec:phase2}). It shows that a support decision timed from recorded history cannot act on a disruption that has not yet happened. It does not show that the disruption leaves no trace.
\item \textbf{\textit{Falsely flagged student.}} This student receives a referral for support on the basis of one weak term despite low genuine underlying vulnerability, consistent with the 25\% false-flag rate of Section~\ref{sec:phase4}. The case shows that even a reasonably well-targeted early-warning mechanism allocates some real support resources to students who did not strictly need them. This operational cost deserves surfacing in any downstream analysis, and the analysis should not assume clean targeting.
\end{enumerate}

The edge-case measure in Table~\ref{tab:validation} checks that both cases actually occur, not merely that they could.

\section{Ethical and Responsible-Use Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

\subsection{No Real Personal Data}
\label{sec:ethics-A}

Every student, term, grade, and event in the model is synthetic. The model uses no real student's record as an input, template, or individual-level validation target. Calibration uses only aggregate, already-published regional and national statistics. Data-privacy obligations that attach to real student records (consent, retention limits, access control) do not apply to the output in the same way, because the output represents no real individual's information.

\subsection{Misrepresentation Risk}
\label{sec:ethics-B}

The principal ethical hazard here is misattribution, not privacy. It consists of presenting a pattern from this synthetic model as an empirical finding about real Philippine students. Any documentation, notebook, or presentation built on the model must state plainly, wherever a number derived from the synthetic data appears, that the number describes the behavior of a generative model calibrated against cited aggregate statistics. It does not measure any real population. The exception is the small set of input statistics in Table~\ref{tab:params} marked CALIBRATED with a citation. Those numbers are real and carry that label.

\subsection{Regional Framing}
\label{sec:ethics-C}

Region names appear in this document only with the real, cited figures published about them (Section~\ref{sec:phase1}, Table~\ref{tab:params}). The model makes or implies no claim beyond those figures about the cause of a region's attrition rate, the quality of its institutions, or the character of its students. Any dissemination of work built on the model should preserve that boundary.

This boundary is necessary but not sufficient. Calibrating a region's high-attrition-tier assignment to a cited figure is a claim about an aggregate statistic. It does not neutralize a separate risk one step downstream, once an implementation generates individual synthetic records. A generator calibrated on BARMM's 93.4\% figure will by construction produce tens of thousands of individually plausible synthetic student narratives. Each narrative combines a low grade, poor attendance, an active financial hold, and eventual dropout, and each carries a tag to that real, specific, historically marginalized, post-conflict region. Aggregate calibration accuracy says nothing about whether generating, retaining, or circulating that volume of synthetic ``at-risk BARMM student'' narratives is responsible. The concern grows when someone disaggregates the records to region level, where a reader could mistake a synthetic profile for a description of real students there. This risk differs from the misattribution risk of Section~\ref{sec:ethics-B} and does not resolve with it. A chart correctly labeled as synthetic can still read as stigmatizing at the individual-record level if someone presents, browses, or distributes it as a set of real case studies. Any implementation of this model should therefore follow four rules. (i) Present synthetic individual-level records, if at all, only with an unambiguous, unavoidable synthetic-data label at the point of display, not only in an appendix or methods section. (ii) Avoid disseminating region-disaggregated individual-level synthetic records beyond the aggregate statistics that the validation checks in Table~\ref{tab:validation} need, since the aggregates carry the calibration value and the individual records add nothing to it. (iii) Avoid pairing a record's region tag with narrative embellishment (a name, an invented life story) beyond the exported tabular fields in Table~\ref{tab:entities}, since embellishment turns a calibration artifact into something that reads as a case study of a real person. (iv) If a presentation needs an illustrative individual example at all, prefer one from the typical tier, or explicitly justify why a region-tagged high-attrition-tier example is necessary. These rules are recommendations for any implementation, not requirements that this document can enforce.

\subsection{Scope of Applicability}
\label{sec:ethics-D}

This model is a methodological exercise and training artifact. It differs in kind from a real institutional early-warning system, it never underwent validation against one, and it cannot substitute for one. Any operational system built with real student data would need its own ethical review, consent framework, and data-protection compliance, none of which this synthetic specification satisfies.

\section{Limitations and Scope}
\label{sec:limitations}

The model's scope is bounded deliberately, and this section states the boundaries instead of leaving them implicit.

\begin{itemize}[topsep=2pt,itemsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{Two-tier regional structure.} The model collapses 17 regions into two tiers. A future iteration with region-specific calibration data could model finer structure.
\item \textbf{Single shock type.} Term shock is one undifferentiated latent event. Real acute disruptions (medical, financial, family) likely differ in frequency and severity, and this version does not distinguish them.
\item \textbf{Single combined intervention.} Support is one undifferentiated flag. It does not separate tutoring, financial counseling, and advising outreach, which likely have different true effect sizes.
\item \textbf{Fixed, not heterogeneous, intervention effect.} Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard} applies the same 30\% relative hazard reduction to every supported student-term, although the paper's own cited related work on individual treatment effects \citep{smith2020global} treats heterogeneity as the object of interest. This scope decision (Section~\ref{sec:phase5}) gives the Recoverability benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:validation}) one unambiguous value. A heterogeneous-effects version is a natural extension.
\item \textbf{Discrete pre-noise risk score.} Because Eq.~\ref{eq:risk}'s three inputs are binary, the pre-noise risk score takes only eight distinct values across the cohort (2 $\times$ 2 $\times$ 2 combinations of low grade, low attendance, and hold status). A real institutional score is finer. The worked example in Section~\ref{sec:phase4} shows that this discreteness pushes calibrated assignment probabilities toward a near-step function even under a formally stochastic mechanism. It also makes assignment nearly deterministic in two of the four signal-count cells, which limits the estimators that can recover the effect (Section~\ref{sec:validation}). A later revision could add a continuous input, such as the overdue amount that FinancialHolds already tracks.
\item \textbf{Cohort alignment of national figures.} Section~\ref{sec:calibration} reads the 29.4\% national figure as a Class of 2023 value from the layout of CPBRD's table. An enrollment-weighted check and CHED's FY2026 budget presentation (which reports the same figure for AY 2023--24) support this reading, but no printed label confirms it. The paper does not tie the 39\% figure to a graduating batch. If the column order were read the other way, the calibration and held-out design of Sections~\ref{sec:calibration} and~\ref{sec:validation} would need redoing. A future revision should confirm the mapping of cohort labels to school years against CHED's underlying data. The typical-tier target of about 46\% also rests on enrollment weights that proxy, rather than equal, cohort sizes. The derived national rate (about 49\%) sits about ten points above the 39\% headline figure.
\item \textbf{Static, cohort-specific calibration.} Regional attrition changes by about 19 percentage points on average between the Class of 2022 and the Class of 2023. The model calibrates to one cohort (the Class of 2022, for which the regional tier structure is fully reported) instead of the most recent, and it does not model trend. Its attrition levels are therefore cohort-specific.
\item \textbf{Attrition is not permanent dropout.} Press coverage of CHED data describes CHED's attrition measure as counting students who temporarily leave as well as those who drop out permanently, computed from the change in enrollment between a batch's first and last years. CPBRD's table defines it as drop-out rates for the whole cohort from first to fourth year. The model's dropout flag has no re-entry, so calibrating to these figures treats temporary stop-outs as dropouts. The calibrated rates measure attrition and give an upper bound on permanent exit.
\item \textbf{Gender, disability, and indigenous status are not modeled.} The cited Philippine qualitative sources \citep{cahilog2025,socioeconomic2024} describe financial and institutional drivers in detail. The studies cited give no quantitative Philippine higher-education attrition breakdown by gender, disability, or indigenous identity, however, that this model could calibrate against as Table~\ref{tab:params} does for region and income. This project's source review located none. Their absence reflects a gap in available calibration data, not a judgment that these factors are unimportant. A future iteration that locates or commissions such a breakdown should add them as Phase 1 attributes that influence baseline vulnerability (Section~\ref{sec:phase2}) and should not treat this version's silence as a finding.
\item \textbf{Reproducibility stated, not guaranteed across platforms.} Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixB} states a seed and versioning policy for 50,000 students over up to eight terms each. Bitwise reproducibility should hold only on a fixed platform and library version, and cross-platform equivalence is statistical, not exact.
\end{itemize}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper specifies a two-layer synthetic data model for student retention in Philippine higher education. A latent baseline vulnerability and an independent per-term shock jointly drive an exported record of grades, attendance, financial-hold status, and intervention history, and no downstream analyst ever sees either latent quantity. The model calibrates its aggregate behavior against national and regional attrition figures from CPBRD, EDCOM~II, and PIDS. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} traces these figures to their sources and graduating cohorts, so the model does not treat them as one undifferentiated body of corroboration. Section~\ref{sec:validation} separates calibration-until-convergence from independent validation and pre-specifies a limited held-out check against a second graduating cohort.

Two equations (Eqs.~\ref{eq:risk}--\ref{eq:intervention}) fully specify the intervention-assignment mechanism, with explicit index ranges, sign constraints, and a worked numeric demonstration. The mechanism is deliberately confounded, which follows the documented statistical failure mode of real early-warning systems. The dataset therefore carries a known ground-truth causal effect, and any downstream analysis can be scored against it, not merely judged plausible. Every numeric parameter carries a tag of fixed by design, calibrated against a cited source, or explicitly assumed. The significance of the model lies in this combination. It gives analysts a testbed where the hidden state and the true effect are known, so they can test whether a retention analysis recovers the truth.

The pilot instantiation (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixA}) supports three conclusions. The mechanism calibrates to its tier targets and generates the intended confounding. The near-deterministic assignment that the targeting figures imply limits valid effect estimation. And the pre-specified held-out check is informative, not automatic, since the pilot lands marginally outside its band. The generator notebook and analysis deliverables that implement and exercise this specification appear separately. This document defines the structure that they build against.

\section{Reference Instantiation and Pilot Check}
\label{sec:appendixA}

This appendix gives one admissible instantiation of the generative forms that Sections~\ref{sec:phase3}--\ref{sec:phase5} leave open, and it reports what a pilot implementation shows. The pilot checks the feasibility of the specification and is not the production generator. It implements only the core mechanism (Phases 1--5) and omits the non-core attributes of Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixB}. Every value below is ASSUMED unless marked as calibrated. A script that reproduces the results accompanies this paper. An implementation may depart from this instantiation if it respects the constraints of Sections~\ref{sec:phase1}--\ref{sec:phase5}.

\subsection{Instantiation}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Entry attributes and baseline vulnerability (Phases 1--2).} $\text{tier}_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.15)$, $\text{work}_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.35)$, and $\text{inc}_i, \varepsilon_i \sim N(0,1)$ independently, where $\text{inc}_i$ is a standardized income proxy whose quantiles define the exported income bracket. Baseline vulnerability $v_i$ is $\delta\,\text{tier}_i + 0.5\,\text{work}_i - 0.4\,\text{inc}_i + 0.8\,\varepsilon_i$, standardized across the cohort to mean 0 and standard deviation 1; $\delta$ is calibrated.
\item \textbf{Term shock.} $\text{shock}_{i,t} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.08)$, independent of every other quantity.
\item \textbf{Observables (Phase 3).} With $e^g, e^a \sim N(0,1)$ independent, $\text{grade}_{i,t} = 72 - 7v_i - 7\,\text{shock}_{i,t} + 8e^g_{i,t}$ and $\text{attendance}_{i,t} = 0.88 - 0.06v_i - 0.08\,\text{shock}_{i,t} + 0.08e^a_{i,t}$ (clipped to $[0,100]$ and $[0,1]$ when exported; clipping does not change the comparisons with $\tau_g$ and $\tau_a$), and $\text{hold}_{i,t} \sim \text{Bernoulli}\big(\sigma(-2.2 + 0.9v_i + 1.2\,\text{shock}_{i,t})\big)$. The three are independent given $(v_i, \text{shock}_{i,t})$, as Section~\ref{sec:phase3} requires.
\item \textbf{Assignment (Phase 4).} Eqs.~\ref{eq:risk}--\ref{eq:intervention} with $\tau_g = 60$, $\tau_a = 0.75$, $\beta_0 = -9$ and $\beta = 6$, and no assignment in term 1.
\item \textbf{Outcome (Phase 5).} The hazard of Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard} uses the fixed values $\gamma_1 = 0.8$ and $\gamma_2 = 1.5$. Calibration then sets $\gamma_0$ and $\delta$ to the two Class of 2022 tier targets, using common random numbers and nested bisection (the typical-tier rate is monotone in $\gamma_0$ and the high-tier rate in $\delta$). The result is $\gamma_0 = -2.67$ and $\delta = 1.06$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reading the results}

Four conclusions follow, each limited by this being one instantiation with illustrative forms. First, the specified mechanism is calibratable, since one free hazard intercept and one tier shift meet two tier targets exactly. Second, the pilot generates confounding at a scale that no one could mistake for the true effect. Third, the targeting figures of Section~\ref{sec:phase4} force near-deterministic assignment in two of the four cells of Table~\ref{tab:positivity}. This is why estimators that need overlap fail and estimators that assume a constant risk ratio succeed. The constant risk ratio is true by construction (Eq.~\ref{eq:hazard}), so this result concerns what the data can identify without that assumption, not the estimators' general merit. It follows from Eqs.~\ref{eq:risk}--\ref{eq:intervention} and Table~\ref{tab:worked} and does not depend on the Phase 3 forms. Fourth, the held-out check is not automatic. The single logit-scale level shift predicts a wider tier gap (1.71) than the data showed between the two cohorts (1.37 on the three high-tier regions with a reported Class of 2023 value). A hazard with this structure is therefore too rigid to reproduce how the gap moved. This is the kind of failure that the check exists to expose. The band was fixed before the pilot ran and has not been adjusted. The result does not show that the architecture is wrong, only that this instantiation does not pass.

\subsection{Integrity rules}

\begingroup\sloppy
\begin{itemize}
\item Baseline vulnerability, the shock indicators, the hazard $h_{i,t}$, and any calibration intermediate are never exported.
\item No panel row exists after a student's dropout term, and every \texttt{student\_id} in a panel table appears in Students.
\item Leakage rule. The generator must not derive any exported variable from the dropout outcome of its own or a later term, or from later-term observables. The non-core attributes (\texttt{program}, \texttt{distance\_km}, the unit counts, \texttt{overdue\_amount}, and the subsidy fields) may depend only on entry attributes, the latent state, and current-term observables; beyond that their marginal forms are unconstrained by this paper.
\item The risk score and the intervention are undefined and false, respectively, in a student's first term (Section~\ref{sec:phase4}).
\end{itemize}
\endgroup

\subsection{Reproducibility policy}

\begingroup\sloppy
\begin{enumerate}
\item Each build uses one integer master seed, recorded in the dataset metadata.
\item Random streams are independent per phase and per variable and derived from the master seed by a documented spawning scheme (for example NumPy's \texttt{SeedSequence}), so adding or changing one variable does not alter the draws of any other.
\item Calibration uses common random numbers, so the uniform and normal draws are held fixed while parameters vary and tier-level attrition is a monotone function of the calibrated parameters.
\item Each release records the specification version, the generator version, the master seed, the library versions, the full tagged parameter log, and a content hash of every exported table.
\item Reproduction is exact only on the same platform and library versions; across platforms, equivalence is statistical, meaning that the checks of Table~\ref{tab:validation} agree within sampling error.
\end{enumerate}
\endgroup
